# Compliance & Risk — gaming and collectibles

Gaming is a high-IP-density category. Most stores that die in this niche die from a
trademark complaint or a payment processor review, not from bad ads. This is the
short version of what to actually do.

## The three risks that end a store

### 1. Payment processor freeze — the fastest killer

Stripe and PayPal run risk reviews triggered by chargebacks, counterfeit complaints,
or a rights-holder report. The outcome is a hold on your balance for 90–180 days.
For a business where supplier payments come out of yesterday's revenue, that's fatal
long before any legal process starts.

Reduce it by: shipping within your stated window, publishing a real returns policy
and honoring it, answering support within 24 hours, and never listing product you
can't prove is authentic.

### 2. Trademark / IP takedown

Rights holders in this space are unusually active. Bandai, Toei, Shueisha, Nintendo,
and The Pokémon Company all run enforcement programs. They target three things:

- the product itself (bootleg figures, logo-molded accessories)
- **your listing images** — a photo you pulled from a supplier that contains
  copyrighted character art is itself infringing
- **your ad creative** — running game footage or character art in a Meta/TikTok ad is
  a separate exposure and gets ad accounts banned

That second and third point catch people who thought they were safe because their
product was generic.

### 3. Copyright — preloaded ROMs

Covered in `avoid-list.md` item 1. Selling handhelds preloaded with unlicensed games
is contributory infringement with real precedent behind it. Sell them blank or don't
sell them.

## Rules to run the store by

**Product naming.** Descriptive compatibility is defensible: "Charging dock —
compatible with PS5 DualSense controllers." Brand-forward naming is not: "PlayStation
5 Official Charging Dock." Never use "official," "genuine," or "licensed" unless it
is, and you can document it.

**Images.** Use your own photos, supplier photos you've confirmed are clean, or
AI/rendered images of the actual product. Never use images containing character art,
game screenshots, or logos you don't have rights to. This is the most commonly
skipped step and one of the most commonly enforced.

**Ad creative.** No game footage, no character art, no franchise music. Shoot the
product on a desk. If you need atmosphere, use your own commissioned art (which is
exactly why Tier D Route 2 in the shortlist is worth doing).

**Supplier claims.** If a Chinese supplier says a figure is "official" or "licensed,"
treat that as meaningless unless there's a distributor agreement behind it. They will
not indemnify you, and a foreign indemnity clause is not practically enforceable
anyway. Authenticity comes from *authorized distribution*, not from a supplier's
product description.

## Counterfeit red flags when sourcing figures

- Price far below comparable listings for the same figure
- Sold "no box" for a line that ships boxed (collector lines always ship boxed)
- Blurry photos, or photos whose lighting and angles don't match each other
- Listing copy that says "official" without naming the license holder
- The same "exclusive" figure available from thirty different sellers at thirty
  different prices

## Product safety and shipping compliance

- **Lithium batteries:** under 100Wh ships normally; above needs UN38.3 documentation
  and hits air-freight restrictions. Check the rating on VR straps, wireless
  peripherals, and anything marketed as a power bank.
- **Electrical certification:** US-market electronics need FCC; EU needs CE/UKCA.
  Request the certificate from your supplier and keep the file.
- **Under-14 marketing:** if you market to minors you inherit CPSIA testing
  obligations in the US. Simplest fix — don't target under-14 in your ad settings.
- **Prop 65 (California):** some cables and PVC goods need a warning label. Ask
  suppliers whether their product carries one.

## Store hygiene that prevents most of this

- Real business entity (LLC), business bank account, business email on a real domain
- Terms of service, privacy policy, shipping policy, and returns policy actually
  published — processors check for these
- Accurate delivery estimates on the product page (under-promise; late delivery is
  the #1 chargeback cause in dropshipping)
- Support inbox answered daily
- A second payment processor set up *before* you need it
